Gantt charts

A Gantt chart gives every task its own row on one time axis, with arrows for the task dependencies between them. It works out which tasks set the finish date, so readers can see what may slip and what may not.

Tasks, milestones, and groups

Each task has a start and either an end or a length. With calendar dates, the end date is included, so 4–10 June is seven days, and a length counts days. A milestone marks a single moment, such as a release, and is drawn as a diamond. The filled part of a bar shows how much of the task is done.

Tasks that share a group appear together under a group row. The group's bar runs from its earliest start to its latest finish, and the arrow beside its name folds it away. The axis can also use plain numbers, such as days from kickoff or weeks of an experiment; put the unit in the caption.

Dependencies

An arrow means one task has to wait for another. Most arrows are finish to start: the next task starts when the previous one finishes. Three other kinds cover work that overlaps. Any of them can add a lag, such as “a day after drafting starts”. When the dates break a dependency, the arrow turns into a dashed orange line and the late task is outlined, so the problem is easy to spot.

KindMeansIn the chart
Finish to startB starts after A finishesFraming waits for the foundation
Start to startB starts after A startsWiring starts two days into framing
Finish to finishB finishes after A finishesThe inspection ends a day after wiring
Start to finishB finishes after A startsSite lighting runs until framing starts

A loop of dependencies, where a task ends up waiting for itself, cannot be scheduled. The chart refuses it, names the loop in the page’s problem report, and keeps the written list on the page instead.

The critical path and float

The critical path is the chain of tasks that sets the finish date: if any of them slips, the finish slips too. The chart works it out from the dates and arrows and outlines those tasks and arrows in red. Every other task has total float, the time it can slip without moving the finish. In the chapter plan, Proof figures has three days of float, while Edit chapter has none. Each row’s hint and the readout name the float.

Plan, progress, and status

A thin grey bar under a task shows when it was planned, so a slipped task stands out at once. The purple as of line marks the date the progress was reported. It is written into the page rather than read from a clock, so the chart says the same thing whenever it is opened.

Reveal a plan step by step

Tasks and layers can appear one step at a time, following a worked-example stepper or other step controls. Here the first step shows the work already agreed, the next adds the review tasks, and the last two bring in the critical path and the status line.

When to reach for it

Use a Gantt chart when tasks wait for each other and the finish date matters. For events in parallel lanes without dependencies, a timeline is lighter, and for a strict order of steps without dates, a pipeline diagram or stepper fits better. Keep it to a few dozen rows; split a larger plan into one chart per phase.

For every reader

Tab reaches the selected row. ↑ and ↓ move between rows, Home and End jump to the ends, and on a group row ← and → fold and unfold it. Every row’s name reads out its dates, progress, dependencies, and float. On a phone the chart scrolls sideways inside the figure. Print shows a list of every row, and with scripts off the written list and every detail stay readable.
