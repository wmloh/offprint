Design with hierarchy

Visual hierarchy: guiding the eye

Visual hierarchy is the order in which a page asks to be read. This page shows the few simple levers that set that order and how to check it with a squint.

Nothing was added except structure. The flat version makes you read every word to learn what matters. The structured version answers three questions at a glance: what this is, what to do, and what could go wrong. That is the whole job of visual hierarchy.

The levers

Every page sets its order with the same handful of levers. Each one makes something either louder or quieter than its neighbours, and DocForm already sets them for you in a calm, consistent way.

LeverWhat it tells the readerWhere to see it here
SizeBigger things are read first and matter more.The page title, then the section headings, then this text.
WeightBold words are the key words in a sentence.The bold step names in the structured demo.
ColourA colour marks meaning, and the same colour always means the same thing.The purple Try it labels always mean something to try.
ContrastStrong differences pull the eye; soft ones step back.Muted labels in the fields under the title, beside darker values.
SpaceA wide gap starts a new topic; a narrow gap keeps things together.The extra room above each section heading.
PositionWhat comes first, and what sits at the left edge, is read first.The short summary right under the title.
GroupingThings that sit together, look alike, or share a box belong together.The coloured line beside each section, and the box around each demo.

Grouping works in three ways. Proximity means nearness: a label sits close to its value. Similarity means a shared look: every tag in one colour has one meaning. Enclosure means a shared box: a callout or figure holds its contents apart from the text around it.

A lever only works by contrast with what surrounds it. One bold word in a sentence stands out; a paragraph of bold words stands out from nothing. That is why DocForm keeps most of the page quiet, so the few loud things can lead.

How readers scan

Most readers do not read a page from top to bottom at first. They scan: the eye jumps to the title, runs down the headings, catches the first words of lines along the left edge, and stops at anything that looks different. Only then does a reader choose a section to read closely.Eye-tracking studies of people reading the web describe this as reading the headings first, like the layers of a cake, and reading less of each line further down the page.

A structured page feeds that habit. Short headings say what each section holds, the first sentence of each section carries its point, and labels and steps break the text into pieces the eye can land on. The outline in the sidebar works the same way: it is the page’s headings, ready to scan.

A flat page has no landing spots. The reader has to hold everything in mind while searching for the one sentence they need, which is slow and tiring. When every part looks equally important, nothing looks important.

The squint test

The squint test checks a page’s hierarchy by blurring it. Squint until the words turn fuzzy, and only the strongest signals survive. If the title, the headings, and one or two key blocks still show through, the page leads the eye. If everything melts into the same grey, nothing does.

Focus mode can do the squinting for you. It softens blocks near the top and bottom edges of the screen that belong to other sections, while the section you are reading stays sharp.

Try it

Turn on focus mode with its button in the control pill or by pressing F. Scroll until the two small pages below just rise above the bottom edge of the screen and turn soft. Keep the pointer away from them, because resting on a block brings it back into focus. Which side still shows where to start? Press F again to return to the regular view.

Two pages, out of focus

Run the same test on any page you make. The parts that survive the blur should be the parts you most want read. If a decoration survives and the main point does not, make the decoration quieter before making the point louder.
