Linear transformations

Definition A linear map T : \R^2 \to \R^2 respects addition and scaling. Every such map is multiplication by a matrix, whose columns are T\vec e_1 and T\vec e_2.

Watching a matrix act

A linear map moves the whole grid at once. Grid lines stay straight, parallel, and evenly spaced, and the origin stays put. Scroll through the steps and watch the unit square and the images of the basis vectors.

Shear. \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} slides each row sideways in proportion to its height. Area is unchanged.

Rotation. A quarter turn, \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, keeps every length and angle.

Scaling. Stretching by 2 and squashing by \tfrac12 keeps the area, since \det = 1.

Reflection. A mirror flips orientation: the orange arrow now sits clockwise from the blue one, and \det = -1.

Identity. Back where we started. Scroll back up to replay the steps in reverse.

The determinant

Area scaling A linear map multiplies every area by |\det A|. When \det A = 0, the map flattens the plane, so it has no inverse.
Why ad - bc?

The unit square maps to the parallelogram with sides (a, c) and (b, d). Its signed area is the 2D cross product of those sides, ad - bc.

Directions that only stretch

Some matrices leave certain directions unchanged and only stretch along them. Such a direction is an eigenvector, and the stretch factor \lambda solves \det(A - \lambda I) = 0. In the shear above, the horizontal axis is one.
