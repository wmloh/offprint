The Pythagorean theorem

Few results are as old or as useful as this one. It links the three sides of every right triangle, and it is the reason distance on a map is computed with a square root.It carries Pythagoras's name, but Babylonian scribes were using it more than a thousand years before him. The proof below needs nothing more than the areas of squares and triangles.

Statement Theorem

Let a right triangle have legs a and b, and let c be the hypotenuse. Then

a^2 + b^2 = c^2.
Reading the squares Each term is the area of a square built on one side. The theorem says the two small squares together cover exactly as much area as the large one.

See it with areas

Step through the figure. The legs measure a = 3 and b = 4, so the hypotenuse is c = 5. builds a square on each side.

Why it holds

  1. Arrange four copies of the triangle inside a square of side a + b.
  2. The space they leave uncovered is a tilted square of side c.
  3. Expanding the left side below and cancelling 2ab leaves the theorem.
(a + b)^2 = 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}ab + c^2
\Longrightarrow\quad a^2 + b^2 = c^2.

Using it

The distance between points (x_1, y_1) and (x_2, y_2) is the hypotenuse of a triangle whose legs are the horizontal and vertical gaps, so givesThe same idea extends to any number of dimensions: add one squared gap per coordinate.

d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}.
The theorem needs a right angle. For other triangles, use the law of cosines, c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos\gamma, which reduces to it when \gamma = 90^\circ.
